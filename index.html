Chapter 01

Mechanics

Motion, forces, momentum, energy, power, moments, circular motion and gravity.

1.1 Scalars and Vectors

+
Scalar: a physical quantity that has magnitude only.
Vector: a physical quantity that has both magnitude and direction.
Scalar Vector
Distance Displacement
Speed Velocity
Mass Force
Energy Acceleration
Time Momentum
Common mistake: Do not automatically assume speed and velocity mean the same thing. Speed has no direction; velocity does.

1.2 Distance and Displacement

+
Distance is the total length of the path travelled.
Displacement is the straight-line change in position from the starting point to the finishing point, including direction.

Imagine walking 5 m east and then 5 m west. Your distance travelled is 10 m, but your displacement is 0 m because you finished where you started.

Memory: Distance asks "how far did you travel?" Displacement asks "where did you finish compared with where you started?"

1.3 Speed, Velocity and Acceleration

+

Speed

speed = distance ÷ time

v = d / t
  • v = speed in m/s
  • d = distance in m
  • t = time in s
Average speed is calculated using total distance divided by total time.

Velocity

velocity = displacement ÷ time

v = s / t

Velocity is speed in a specified direction.

Acceleration

a = (v - u) / t
  • a = acceleration in m/s²
  • v = final velocity in m/s
  • u = initial velocity in m/s
  • t = time in seconds
Worked Example
A car increases its velocity from 10 m/s to 30 m/s in 5 seconds.

a = (v - u) / t

a = (30 - 10) / 5

a = 4 m/s²

This means the velocity increases by 4 m/s every second.

Common mistake: Always convert units before substituting. For example, 72 km/h is not 72 m/s.
Memory: Acceleration tells you how quickly velocity changes.

1.4 Equations of Motion

+

For constant acceleration, the standard equations of motion are extremely important.

v = u + at
s = ut + ½at²
v² = u² + 2as
s = ½(u + v)t
Symbol Meaning Unit
uInitial velocitym/s
vFinal velocitym/s
aAccelerationm/s²
sDisplacementm
tTimes

How to choose the equation

Look at which quantities you have and which quantity you need. Choose an equation that contains those quantities and does not introduce an unnecessary unknown.

Example
An object starts from rest and accelerates at 3 m/s² for 4 seconds. Given:
u = 0 m/s
a = 3 m/s²
t = 4 s

We want v, so use:

v = u + at

v = 0 + (3)(4)

v = 12 m/s
Important: These equations assume constant acceleration.

1.5 Motion Graphs

+

Distance-Time Graph

  • Gradient represents speed.
  • A horizontal line means the object is stationary.
  • A steeper line means greater speed.

Velocity-Time Graph

  • Gradient represents acceleration.
  • Horizontal line means constant velocity.
  • Area under the graph represents displacement.

Acceleration-Time Graph

  • The vertical value represents acceleration.
  • Area under the graph represents change in velocity.
Graph memory:
Distance-time → gradient = speed
Velocity-time → gradient = acceleration
Velocity-time → area = displacement

1.6 Newton's Laws of Motion

+

Newton's First Law

An object remains at rest or continues moving with constant velocity unless acted upon by a resultant external force.

This is the principle of inertia. An object does not need a force to continue moving at constant velocity. A resultant force is required to change its motion.

Newton's Second Law

The resultant force on an object is proportional to its acceleration, with the relationship depending on the object's mass.
F = ma
  • F = resultant force in newtons (N)
  • m = mass in kilograms (kg)
  • a = acceleration in m/s²

Newton's Third Law

When object A exerts a force on object B, object B exerts an equal and opposite force on object A.
Important: The two forces act on different objects. Therefore they do not simply cancel each other.

1.7 Weight, Mass and Gravity

+
Mass is the amount of matter in an object and is measured in kilograms.
Weight is the gravitational force acting on an object.
W = mg
  • W = weight in N
  • m = mass in kg
  • g = gravitational acceleration in m/s²

Near Earth's surface, g is approximately 9.8 m/s².

Worked Example
A 60 kg person stands on Earth.

W = mg

W = 60 × 9.8

W = 588 N

1.8 Momentum and Impulse

+
Momentum is the product of an object's mass and velocity.
p = mv

Momentum is measured in kg m/s.

Conservation of Momentum

In an isolated system, total momentum before an interaction equals total momentum after the interaction.
total momentum before = total momentum after

Impulse

Impulse = Ft = change in momentum
FΔt = Δp

Increasing the time over which an object is brought to rest reduces the average force for the same change in momentum.

Real-world application
Car airbags increase the time over which the passenger's momentum changes. Because the change in momentum is the same, increasing the stopping time reduces the average force.

1.9 Work, Energy and Power

+

Work

Work is done when a force causes displacement in the direction of the force.
W = Fs

W is measured in joules.

Kinetic Energy

Ek = ½mv²

Gravitational Potential Energy

Ep = mgh

Power

Power is the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred.
P = W/t
P = E/t

Power is measured in watts. 1 watt = 1 joule per second.

Efficiency

Efficiency = useful output energy / total input energy × 100%
Common mistake: Energy is measured in joules. Power is measured in watts. They are not the same quantity.

1.10 Moments and Equilibrium

+
The moment of a force about a point is the turning effect of the force about that point.
Moment = force × perpendicular distance from pivot
M = Fd

Principle of Moments

For an object in rotational equilibrium, the sum of clockwise moments about a point equals the sum of anticlockwise moments about that point.
clockwise moments = anticlockwise moments

Conditions for Equilibrium

For an object to be completely in equilibrium:

  • The resultant force must be zero.
  • The resultant moment must be zero.

1.11 Circular Motion

+

An object moving in a circle is continually changing direction, so its velocity is changing even if its speed remains constant.

Centripetal force is the resultant force directed towards the centre of a circular path.
Fc = mv²/r

The corresponding centripetal acceleration is:

ac = v²/r
Important: Centripetal force is not necessarily a new type of force. It is the name given to the resultant inward force. It may be provided by tension, gravity, friction or another force.

1.12 Universal Gravitation

+
Every mass attracts every other mass with a force that is proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between their centres.
F = Gm₁m₂/r²
  • F = gravitational force
  • G = universal gravitational constant
  • m₁ and m₂ = masses
  • r = distance between their centres
Inverse-square idea: If distance doubles, gravitational force becomes one quarter as large.
Chapter 02

Thermal Physics

Temperature, heat, energy transfer, changes of state and gases.

2.1 Temperature and Heat

+
Temperature is a measure related to the average kinetic energy of the particles in a substance.
Heat is energy transferred from a hotter object to a colder object because of a temperature difference.

Heat is energy in transfer. It is not simply something that an object "contains" in the same way that it contains mass.

Temperature Scales

Temperature in thermodynamic calculations is often measured in kelvin.

T(K) = T(°C) + 273.15

2.2 Specific Heat Capacity

+
Specific heat capacity is the amount of energy required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 K.
Q = mcΔT
  • Q = heat energy in J
  • m = mass in kg
  • c = specific heat capacity in J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹
  • ΔT = temperature change in K or °C
Worked Example
2 kg of a material has a specific heat capacity of 500 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. Its temperature increases by 10 K.

Q = mcΔT

Q = 2 × 500 × 10

Q = 10,000 J
Common mistake: Mass must be in kilograms, not grams.

2.3 Specific Latent Heat

+
Specific latent heat is the energy required to change the state of 1 kg of a substance without changing its temperature.
Q = ml

There are two major types:

  • Specific latent heat of fusion — solid ↔ liquid.
  • Specific latent heat of vaporisation — liquid ↔ gas.

During a change of state, the supplied energy is used to change the arrangement/bonding of particles rather than increasing their temperature.

2.4 Heat Transfer

+

Conduction

Energy is transferred through a material by interactions between particles. In metals, free electrons are especially important in thermal conduction.

Convection

Convection occurs in fluids. Warmer fluid becomes less dense and rises, while cooler, denser fluid falls, producing a convection current.

Radiation

Thermal radiation is electromagnetic radiation. It does not require a medium and can travel through a vacuum.

Memory:
Conduction → contact
Convection → moving fluid
Radiation → electromagnetic waves

2.5 Gas Laws

+

Boyle's Law

For a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature, pressure is inversely proportional to volume.
PV = constant
P₁V₁ = P₂V₂

Charles' Law

For a fixed mass of gas at constant pressure, volume is directly proportional to absolute temperature.
V/T = constant

Pressure Law

For a fixed mass of gas at constant volume, pressure is directly proportional to absolute temperature.
P/T = constant
Important: Gas-law temperatures must normally be in kelvin.
Chapter 03

Waves

Wave properties, reflection, refraction, diffraction and interference.

3.1 What is a Wave?

+
A wave is a disturbance that transfers energy from one location to another without there being a net transfer of matter from one location to another.

Transverse Waves

The particles vibrate perpendicular to the direction in which the wave travels.

Examples include electromagnetic waves.

Longitudinal Waves

The particles vibrate parallel to the direction in which the wave travels.

Sound is a longitudinal wave in air.

3.2 Frequency, Period and Wavelength

+
Frequency is the number of complete waves passing a point per second.
f = 1/T

Frequency is measured in hertz (Hz).

Wavelength is the distance between corresponding points on successive waves, such as crest-to-crest.
v = fλ
  • v = wave speed
  • f = frequency
  • λ = wavelength
Worked Example
A wave has frequency 50 Hz and wavelength 2 m.

v = fλ

v = 50 × 2

v = 100 m/s

3.3 Reflection — Both Laws

+

Reflection occurs when a wave reaches a boundary and is returned into the original medium.

Law 1 of Reflection: The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection.
i = r
Law 2 of Reflection: The incident ray, reflected ray and the normal all lie in the same plane.

Important terminology

  • Incident ray: the ray travelling towards the boundary.
  • Reflected ray: the ray travelling away after reflection.
  • Normal: a line perpendicular to the boundary.
  • Angle of incidence: angle between incident ray and normal.
  • Angle of reflection: angle between reflected ray and normal.
Major exam mistake: Angles of incidence and reflection are measured from the normal, not from the surface.

3.4 Refraction — Both Laws

+

Refraction is the change in direction of a wave when it passes from one medium into another because its speed changes.

Law 1 of Refraction: The incident ray, refracted ray and the normal at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane.
Law 2 of Refraction — Snell's Law: For two particular media, the ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence to the sine of the angle of refraction is constant.
n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r

For light travelling from air into a material, this is commonly simplified using the refractive index of the material.

n = sin i / sin r

Refractive Index and Speed

n = c/v
  • n = refractive index
  • c = speed of light in vacuum
  • v = speed of light in the material

Which way does the ray bend?

When light enters a medium in which it travels more slowly, it bends towards the normal.

When it enters a medium in which it travels faster, it bends away from the normal.

Important: The frequency of the light does not change when it crosses the boundary. Its speed and wavelength change.

3.5 Diffraction

+
Diffraction is the spreading or bending of waves when they pass through a gap or around an obstacle.

Diffraction is most noticeable when the size of the gap or obstacle is similar to the wavelength.

Key idea: Gap size ≈ wavelength → strong diffraction.

3.6 Interference and Superposition

+
Principle of Superposition: When two or more waves overlap, the resultant displacement is the algebraic sum of the individual displacements.

Constructive Interference

Waves arrive in phase and reinforce each other.

Destructive Interference

Waves arrive out of phase and partially or completely cancel.

Chapter 04

Sound

Sound waves, resonance, standing waves, pitch, loudness and Doppler effect.

4.1 Properties of Sound

+

Sound is a mechanical wave, meaning it requires a medium through which to travel.

In air, sound travels as a longitudinal wave consisting of compressions and rarefactions.

  • Frequency determines pitch.
  • Amplitude is related to loudness.
  • Wave speed depends on the medium.
Remember: Sound cannot travel through a vacuum because there are no particles available to transmit the mechanical disturbance.

4.2 Resonance

+
Resonance occurs when an object is forced to vibrate at or near its natural frequency, producing a large amplitude of vibration.

Every vibrating system has one or more natural frequencies. When the driving frequency matches a natural frequency, energy transfer can become particularly effective.

4.3 Standing Waves

+
A standing wave is formed when two waves of the same frequency, amplitude and speed travel in opposite directions and interfere.
  • Nodes: points of minimum displacement.
  • Antinodes: points of maximum displacement.

Standing waves are important in musical instruments and resonance experiments.

4.4 Doppler Effect

+
The Doppler effect is the apparent change in frequency of a wave caused by relative motion between the source and observer.

If the source approaches the observer, the observed frequency increases.

If the source moves away, the observed frequency decreases.

The same principle applies to sound and electromagnetic radiation.

Chapter 05

Light & Optics

Reflection, refraction, lenses, refractive index and total internal reflection.

5.1 Electromagnetic Spectrum

+

Electromagnetic waves are transverse waves consisting of oscillating electric and magnetic fields.

They can travel through a vacuum.

Region Typical idea
RadioLongest wavelengths
MicrowaveCommunications and heating
InfraredThermal radiation
VisibleDetected by human eyes
UltravioletHigher frequency than visible light
X-raysHigh-energy electromagnetic radiation
GammaHighest-frequency region of the list
c = fλ

All electromagnetic waves travel at the same speed in vacuum: approximately 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s.

5.2 Critical Angle

+
The critical angle is the angle of incidence in the optically denser medium for which the angle of refraction in the less dense medium is 90°.
sin C = 1/n

This form applies when light travels from the material into air or approximately from the denser material into vacuum.

Total Internal Reflection

Two conditions must be satisfied:

  1. Light must travel from an optically denser medium to an optically less dense medium.
  2. The angle of incidence must be greater than the critical angle.
Remember TIR: Denser → less dense + i > C

5.3 Lenses

+

Convex Lens

A convex lens is thicker in the middle and causes parallel rays to converge towards a focal point.

Concave Lens

A concave lens is thinner in the middle and causes parallel rays to diverge.

Lens Formula

1/f = 1/u + 1/v
  • f = focal length
  • u = object distance
  • v = image distance

Magnification

m = image height / object height
m = v/u
Exam tip: Draw a ray diagram when a question involves image formation. It often makes the answer much easier to reason through.
Chapter 06

Electricity

Charge, current, voltage, resistance, circuits, power and electrical energy.

6.1 Electric Charge and Current

+
Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge.
I = Q/t
  • I = current in amperes
  • Q = charge in coulombs
  • t = time in seconds

Conventional current is considered to flow from positive to negative through the external circuit.

6.2 Potential Difference

+
Potential difference is the energy transferred per unit charge between two points in a circuit.
V = W/Q
  • V = potential difference in volts
  • W = energy transferred in joules
  • Q = charge in coulombs

6.3 Ohm's Law

+
For a conductor at constant physical conditions, the current through it is directly proportional to the potential difference across it.
V = IR
  • V = voltage
  • I = current
  • R = resistance
Worked Example
A resistor of 6 Ω carries a current of 2 A.

V = IR

V = 2 × 6

V = 12 V

6.4 Series and Parallel Circuits

+

Series

  • Same current through each component.
  • Voltages add.
  • Total resistance is the sum of resistances.
Rtotal = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + ...

Parallel

  • Same potential difference across each branch.
  • Currents divide between branches.
  • Total current is the sum of branch currents.
1/Rtotal = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃ + ...
Series: current same.
Parallel: voltage same.

6.5 Electrical Power and Energy

+
P = VI

Using Ohm's law, this can also be written as:

P = I²R
P = V²/R

Electrical Energy

E = Pt

Energy is measured in joules when power is in watts and time is in seconds.

6.6 Kirchhoff's Laws

+

Kirchhoff's First Law

At a junction in a circuit, total current entering equals total current leaving.
ΣI(in) = ΣI(out)

Kirchhoff's Second Law

Around any closed loop in a circuit, the algebraic sum of the potential differences is zero.

This follows from conservation of energy.

Chapter 07

Magnetism

Magnetic fields, forces on currents and motor principles.

7.1 Magnetic Fields

+
A magnetic field is a region where a magnetic pole or moving charge experiences a magnetic force.

Magnetic field lines show the direction in which a north magnetic pole would move.

  • Outside a magnet, field lines go from north to south.
  • Field lines never cross.
  • Closer field lines represent a stronger field.

7.2 Force on a Current-Carrying Conductor

+

A current-carrying conductor placed in a magnetic field can experience a force.

F = BIL sin θ

For a conductor perpendicular to the magnetic field:

F = BIL
  • B = magnetic flux density
  • I = current
  • L = length in the field

The direction can be determined using the appropriate motor-force hand rule.

7.3 Electric Motors

+

An electric motor converts electrical energy into mechanical energy.

A current-carrying coil in a magnetic field experiences forces on opposite sides of the coil. These forces produce a turning effect.

The commutator reverses the current every half-turn so the torque continues to act in the same rotational direction.

Chapter 08

Electromagnetic Induction

Generators, transformers and induced voltage.

8.1 Electromagnetic Induction

+
Electromagnetic induction is the production of an emf in a conductor when the magnetic flux linking the conductor changes.
The direction of an induced current is such that its magnetic effect opposes the change that produced it.

This is commonly known as Lenz's law.

A changing magnetic field is therefore essential. Simply having a magnet near a stationary coil does not necessarily produce a continuous current.

8.2 Generators

+

A generator converts mechanical energy into electrical energy using electromagnetic induction.

A coil rotates in a magnetic field, causing the magnetic flux through the coil to change and inducing an emf.

AC Generator

An AC generator uses slip rings to allow the output to alternate direction.

8.3 Transformers

+

A transformer changes an alternating voltage using electromagnetic induction.

Vp/Vs = Np/Ns
  • Vp = primary voltage
  • Vs = secondary voltage
  • Np = primary turns
  • Ns = secondary turns

Step-Up Transformer

Secondary has more turns than primary, so the output voltage is higher.

Step-Down Transformer

Secondary has fewer turns than primary, so the output voltage is lower.

Important: A conventional transformer requires alternating current because a changing magnetic field is needed.
Chapter 09

Modern Physics

Atoms, photons, quantum ideas, radioactivity and nuclear energy.

9.1 Structure of the Atom

+

An atom contains a small dense nucleus surrounded by electrons.

Particle Charge Relative mass
Proton +1 ≈ 1
Neutron 0 ≈ 1
Electron -1 Very small
The atomic number is the number of protons in the nucleus.
The mass number is the total number of protons and neutrons.

9.2 Photons and Quantum Energy

+
A photon is a quantum or packet of electromagnetic radiation.
E = hf

Using c = fλ:

E = hc/λ
  • E = photon energy
  • h = Planck constant
  • f = frequency
  • c = speed of light
  • λ = wavelength

Higher frequency means higher photon energy. Shorter wavelength also means higher photon energy.

9.3 Photoelectric Effect

+
The photoelectric effect is the emission of electrons from a material when electromagnetic radiation of sufficient frequency falls on it.

The key idea is that light transfers energy in individual photons.

Ephoton = hf

If the photon energy is below the required threshold, increasing the intensity alone does not make each photon energetic enough to eject an electron.

9.4 Radioactivity

+
Radioactivity is the spontaneous disintegration of unstable atomic nuclei, with the emission of radiation.

Alpha

An alpha particle consists of two protons and two neutrons. It has a +2 charge.

Beta

Beta radiation involves the emission of a high-speed electron or positron during nuclear transformation.

Gamma

Gamma radiation is high-energy electromagnetic radiation emitted from the nucleus.

Radiation Penetration Ionising ability
Alpha Low High
Beta Medium Medium
Gamma High Lower than alpha

9.5 Half-Life

+
Half-life is the time required for half of the radioactive nuclei in a sample to decay.

After one half-life remains 1/2 of the original amount.

After two half-lives remains 1/4.

After three half-lives remains 1/8.

N = N₀(1/2)ⁿ

where n is the number of half-lives elapsed.

Important: Radioactive decay is random for an individual nucleus, but predictable statistically for a large sample.

9.6 Nuclear Fission and Fusion

+

Fission

A heavy nucleus splits into smaller nuclei, releasing energy and usually additional neutrons.

A chain reaction can occur if released neutrons cause further fissions.

Fusion

Light nuclei combine to form a heavier nucleus, releasing energy.

Fusion powers stars.

Memory: Fission = split.
Fusion = join.

9.7 Mass-Energy Equivalence

+
E = mc²

Mass can be considered a form of stored energy. Because c² is extremely large, a small mass difference can correspond to a very large amount of energy.

Chapter 10

Particle Physics

Fundamental particles, quarks, leptons and conservation principles.

10.1 Fundamental Particles

+

The Standard Model describes matter in terms of fundamental particles and the interactions between them.

Quarks

The six quark flavours are:

up, down, charm, strange, top, bottom

Leptons

The charged leptons include:

electron, muon, tau

There are also corresponding neutrinos.

Hadrons

Hadrons are particles made from quarks.

  • Protons and neutrons are baryons.
  • Mesons consist of a quark and antiquark.

10.2 Conservation Laws

+

Particle interactions are constrained by conservation laws. Depending on the process, quantities such as energy, momentum and charge must be conserved.

Exam approach: When checking whether a particle reaction is possible, compare conserved quantities before and after the interaction.
Chapter 11

Astrophysics

Stars, galaxies, redshift, Hubble's law and the expanding universe.

11.1 Stars

+

Stars are enormous bodies of plasma held together by gravity. Their energy comes primarily from nuclear fusion in their interiors.

The evolution of a star depends strongly on its initial mass.

Basic stellar sequence

  1. A cloud of gas and dust collapses under gravity.
  2. The forming object becomes a protostar.
  3. Fusion begins when sufficient temperature and pressure are reached.
  4. The star enters a stable main-sequence phase.
  5. Its later evolution depends on its mass.

11.2 Spectra

+

Light from stars can be analysed using spectroscopy. Spectral lines provide information about composition, temperature and motion.

An emission spectrum contains wavelengths emitted by excited atoms.
An absorption spectrum contains dark lines where particular wavelengths have been absorbed.

11.3 Redshift

+
Redshift is the shift of spectral features towards longer wavelengths.

For distant galaxies, redshift is evidence that many galaxies are moving away from us as the universe expands.

11.4 Hubble's Law

+
v = H₀d
  • v = recession velocity
  • H₀ = Hubble constant
  • d = distance

The relationship provides evidence for an expanding universe.

11.5 Big Bang Model

+

The Big Bang model describes the universe as having evolved from an extremely hot, dense early state.

Important evidence includes:

  • Expansion of the universe.
  • Cosmic microwave background radiation.
  • Observed abundance of light elements.
Cosmic microwave background radiation is electromagnetic radiation left over from the early universe, now observed mainly in the microwave region.
Chapter 12

Experimental Physics

How to approach practical work, measurements, graphs and uncertainty.

12.1 Variables

+
Independent variable: the quantity you deliberately change.
Dependent variable: the quantity you measure as a result.
Controlled variable: a quantity kept constant so it does not affect the investigation.
Example
If investigating how wire length affects resistance:

Independent variable → length of wire
Dependent variable → resistance
Controlled variables → material, cross-sectional area, temperature

12.2 Accuracy and Precision

+
Accuracy describes how close a measurement is to the accepted or true value.
Precision describes how closely repeated measurements agree with each other.

A set of measurements can be precise but inaccurate if there is a systematic error.

12.3 Experimental Graphs

+

A good experimental graph should normally include:

  • Clearly labelled axes.
  • Units on the axes.
  • A suitable scale.
  • Points plotted accurately.
  • A best-fit line or curve where appropriate.
  • A gradient calculated using well-separated points.

Gradient

gradient = change in y / change in x

The gradient often represents a physical quantity. Always examine the equation behind the experiment to determine what the gradient means.

12.4 Sources of Experimental Error

+

Random Error

Random errors cause readings to vary unpredictably. Repeating measurements and calculating an average can reduce their effect.

Systematic Error

A systematic error shifts measurements consistently in one direction. Repeating the measurement does not automatically remove it.

Improving an experiment

  • Repeat measurements.
  • Calculate averages.
  • Use more precise measuring instruments.
  • Reduce parallax error.
  • Control temperature where relevant.
  • Use a larger range of measurements.
  • Take readings carefully and consistently.
Chapter 13

Exam Skills & Formula Strategy

How to actually use Physics knowledge in calculation and theory questions.

13.1 The Universal Calculation Method

+
  1. Read the entire question. Do not immediately grab a formula.
  2. Write down the known quantities. Give every value its symbol and unit.
  3. Identify what the question asks for. Write the unknown.
  4. Choose the formula. Choose one containing the known values and the unknown.
  5. Rearrange before substituting if necessary. This reduces mistakes.
  6. Substitute with units. Make sure SI units are being used where required.
  7. Calculate. Keep sensible significant figures.
  8. Give the unit. A numerical answer without the correct unit may lose marks.
Example Strategy
Question: A 5 kg object accelerates at 4 m/s². Find the resultant force.

Known:
m = 5 kg
a = 4 m/s²

Unknown:
F = ?

Formula:
F = ma

Substitute:
F = 5 × 4

Answer:
F = 20 N

13.4 Common Physics Mistakes

+
  • Using kilometres instead of metres.
  • Using grams instead of kilograms.
  • Using hours instead of seconds.
  • Forgetting units.
  • Using Celsius instead of kelvin in a gas-law calculation.
  • Confusing mass with weight.
  • Confusing speed with velocity.
  • Measuring optical angles from the surface instead of the normal.
  • Forgetting that current is the same in a series circuit.
  • Forgetting that voltage is the same across parallel branches.
  • Using an equation of motion when acceleration is not constant.
  • Forgetting that centripetal force points towards the centre.
  • Thinking a larger resistance automatically means a larger current.
  • Forgetting that frequency stays constant when light crosses a boundary.
